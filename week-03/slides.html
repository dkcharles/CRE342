02

Rewards
& loops

The motivation engine: what you give, when, and how often

Hallford & Hallford (2001)

Four things a reward can give

GloryRecognition

Achievements, titles, a high score. Changes nothing in play; tells everyone you did it.

SustenanceKeep going

Health, ammo, armour, extra lives. Keeps what you have.

AccessNew places

Keys, unlocked areas, new levels. Opens content you couldn't reach.

FacilityNew powers

Double jump, a new weapon, bigger inventory. Lets you do more.

Rewards also carry information ("you did that right") and set the pace of the game.

Time frame

Short-term and long-term rewards

Short-term rewards keep the moment moving. Long-term rewards give the session a reason.

Structure

Tiered and milestone rewards

Tiered · rewards grow with the level

Bronze → silver → gold. Basic to advanced gear. Currency worth more at each tier.

  • Clear criteria for moving up a tier
  • Each tier clearly better than the last
  • Visible progress: badges, bars, ranks
Milestone · reward for reaching a point

Finish a chapter, reach a score, defeat a major boss.

  • Defined and told to the player in advance
  • Meaningful: worth the effort it took
  • Tied to the story so it deepens immersion

Together they give players goals to set and hit, which is what long-term engagement is made of.

Keeping it going

Motivation has to be sustained

01

Novelty vs familiarity

New content, rewards and challenges on top of core systems that stay the same.

02

Long-term progression

Seasons, mastery ranks, reputations: something to aim at next month.

03

Pacing, not flooding

Too many rewards and each one means nothing; too few and players burn out.

=

Sustainable design

Players stay and are glad they did: retention plus satisfaction.

Retention on its own isn't the goal. A slot machine has great retention.

Reward loops

The core engagement cycle

Act, get rewarded, want to act again. Every game runs several of these loops at once, nested inside each other.

The small loops keep your hands busy. The big loops give the small ones a purpose.

Reinforcement schedules · Week 1's grid, applied

Fixed or random?

Fixed · predictable

Level-up bonuses, quest rewards, achievement unlocks.

  • Clear goals and expectations
  • Easier to balance progression
  • Can feel monotonous if overused
  • Less excitement
Random · unpredictable

Random drops, loot boxes, gacha, surprise events.

  • Surprise, curiosity, replayability
  • Very high engagement
  • Feels unfair after a bad streak
  • Gambling-like when money is involved

Continuous reinforcement (every time) is best for teaching a new behaviour.

Partial reinforcement (sometimes) keeps a behaviour going longest.

03

Feedback

The bridge between what the player does and what the system says back

Input → response

Every input needs an answer

Good

Responsive, clear, consistent. Jump always jumps, now. Builds trust and supports flow.

Poor

Lag, unclear outcomes. "Did that hit?" Players blame the game, then leave it.

Six kinds of feedback

What is the feedback for?

Immediate

Confirms the input landed.

hit marker · button click

Delayed

Reflection after the moment.

end-of-match stats · summaries

Positive

Reinforces what worked.

XP pop · combo counter

Negative

Signals a mistake so you adjust.

health loss · red flash

Informational

Teaches and guides.

"too heavy" · map marker

Aesthetic

Makes it feel good and deepens immersion.

particles · music swell
Worked example · Super Mario Bros

One coin, five layers

LayerWhat happensJob
AudioThe "bling": instant and unmistakableimmediate · aesthetic
MotionThe coin pops out of the block and spinsimmediate · aesthetic
UICoin counter +1, score +200informational · positive
ProgressEvery 100 coins: an extra lifelong-term · sustenance
TeachingCoin trails show where to jumpinformational · guiding

A fast loop (the coin) feeding a slow loop (the 1-up), with every sense saying the same thing.

Audio-visual feedback design

Layer the senses, and design for everyone

Visual

See it

Animations, particle effects, UI cues, screen shake.

Audio

Hear it

Success chimes, failure sounds, narration, music changes.

Multisensory

Layer it

Cues that agree across senses and haptics are stronger and more immersive.

Accessibility

Offer it twice

Colour-blind modes, captions, haptics. Never one channel only.

Watch: Juice it or lose it (Jonasson & Purho, 2012): the same game, with and without feedback.

04

Gamification

Taking game elements out of games

Deterding et al. (2011)

“The use of game design elements in non-game contexts”

Game designGamification
PurposeImmersive, entertaining experiencesEngagement and motivation in something that isn't a game
WhereComplete games, played for their own sakeEducation, fitness, marketing, training, productivity
ElementsFull mechanics, narrative, interactive playSelected elements (points, badges, leaderboards) added to an existing activity

Duolingo, Strava, loyalty cards, and this module's weekly quizzes.

The usual toolkit

Points, badges, leaderboards (PBL)

Points

A number for progress

Awarded for tasks and milestones. Immediate feedback and a sense of accomplishment.

Badges

A symbol of achievement

Earned for goals, challenges and expertise. A status symbol that points to mastery.

Leaderboards

A ranking against others

Competition and social comparison. Motivates the people near the top.

The catch: a global leaderboard motivates the top ten and quietly tells everyone else they're losing. Fixes: show players near you, friends only, weekly resets, small leagues.

Beyond PBL

Challenges, quests and social play

Challenges

Test a skill

Often timed or with a target. Push people to their limits, and give achievement when they get there.

Quests

Give direction

Structured missions with a clear objective. Add purpose and a bit of narrative to routine tasks.

Social

Involve others

Sharing, teams, communities. Collaboration and belonging, not just competition.

These reach the intrinsic needs from Week 1: competence, autonomy, relatedness. PBL mostly doesn't.

McGinnis et al. (2008)

Six essential engaging factors

Fun contains everything. Structure holds challenge and feedback. Social and identity sit alongside.

Use it as a checklist: a design that's missing one of the six usually feels it.

Ethics &
dark patterns

When the reward system works for the business and against the player

Week 1's temporal, monetary and social patterns, in reward design

Five reward dark patterns

Loot boxes

Paid random rewards on a variable-ratio schedule.

Overwatch (2016) · CS:GO keys · FIFA packs

Pay to win

Money buys a competitive edge over players who don't pay.

Clash of Clans gems · Diablo Immortal

Grind walls

Progress slowed on purpose, with a paid way round.

Diablo Immortal · FUT squad challenges

Scarcity & urgency

Countdowns and "limited time" push impulse buys.

Fortnite Item Shop · battle-pass timers

Forced continuity

Free trial that renews at full price unless you cancel.

£1 first month subscriptions · VIP trials

Short-term revenue, long-term cost: lost trust, bad press and regulation.

Case study

Loot boxes

Why they work

Variable-ratio reward, a near-miss when you get a duplicate, and a public reveal. Structurally, they look like gambling (Drummond & Sauer, 2018).

The concern

Loot box spending is linked to problem gambling severity (Zendle & Cairns, 2018). The heaviest spenders are the most vulnerable players.

Fairer alternatives

Cosmetic only · visible odds · earnable in play · buy the item directly · "pity" guarantees.

How regulators responded
2017

China and Apple's App Store require published drop odds.

2018

Belgium rules paid loot boxes are gambling and bans them.

2020

PEGI adds "In-game purchases (includes random items)".

2022

UK decides not to legislate and asks the industry to regulate itself; Overwatch 2 drops loot boxes for a shop and battle pass.

2023

UK industry principles: odds disclosed, parental consent for under-18s.

Designing ethically

Avoiding manipulative design

01

Respect agency

No coercive paywalls. Players' time and choices are valued, not forced.

02

Engage sustainably

Build on fun, mastery and community, not compulsion.

03

Be transparent

Clear rules, visible odds, honest prices.

04

Protect players

Fairness and inclusion; extra care for children and vulnerable players.

The test: would the player still choose this if they fully understood what it was doing?

Take away

Five things to remember

Rewards give glory, sustenance, access or facility, over the short or long term, in tiers and milestones.

Loops nest: fast loops keep momentum, slow loops give them purpose. Fixed rewards teach, random ones hook.

Feedback must be immediate, clear, layered and accessible. Every input needs an answer.

Gamification is more than PBL. Challenge, quests, social play and identity reach intrinsic motivation.

Would they choose it if they understood it? If not, it's a dark pattern.

Roll for
Reward

A dice game in four rounds. Each round adds a new kind of reward.

Set-up · 3 minutes

Tables of 4–5

Kit per table
  • Two six-sided dice in different colours: the main die and the modifier
  • One eight-sided die (no d8? roll a d6 and add 2)
  • About 15 duck tokens: counters, coins or paper squares
  • A score sheet and pen each
How a round works

Take turns clockwise. Everyone gets 5 turns in Rounds 1–2 and 6 turns in Rounds 3–4. Highest score at the table wins the round and gets a ★.

After every round

Rate it on your sheet, on your own: fun (1–7) and I wanted another turn (1–7).

Rather not play? Be the table's scorekeeper and watch for the moments people get excited.

Round 1 · Fixed

Roll the main die. Score what you roll.

1–6

Every roll scores its face value, straight away.

5 turns

Add up your five rolls. Highest at the table gets the ★.

No choices

Nothing to decide. Just roll.

Average score: about 17. Nobody can do much about it.

Round 2 · Variety

Now what you roll decides what you get

Main dieWhat happens
1Tier up: no points, but roll the d8 as your main die for the rest of the round
2, 4Score it
3, 5Score it, then roll the modifier: 4–6 add it, 1–3 take it away
6Take a duck. No points now.
7, 8d8 only: score it
Ducks · scored at the end of the round

4 each

Own three or more and every duck is worth 5.

Ducks stay yours: nothing takes them away.

Round 3 · Risk

Round 2 rules, plus a pot you can lose

Pot · at risk

Points you roll go into your pot. Roll a 1 (on either main die) and you bust: lose the whole pot. You still tier up.

→
Bank · safe

On your turn you can bank instead of rolling: move your pot into your bank. Banked points can't be lost.

6 turns. A bank uses a turn.

End of round: bank + pot + ducks.

Ducks are always safe.

Round 4 · Spotlight

Round 3 rules. Now the whole room is watching.

LeaderboardClass-wide

Scores go up on screen against every table, not just yours.

WinnerTop of the room

The top score in the class is announced as the winner. Ties: a roll-off.

Same dice, same rules. Does it feel different?

The design, read as a design

What each round adds
